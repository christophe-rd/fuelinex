Climate change shifts the timing of their plants' seasonal activities, such as when the leaves emerge in the spring and when they fall in autumn. One of the expected knock-on effects is increased tree growth with longer growing seasons since trees can grow for a longer period. This matters because tree growth is a major part of how forests pull carbon out of the atmosphere, and thus massively slow down the consequences of anthropogenic climate change. But several recent studies failed to connect growth and longer growing seasons, suggesting that other factors-like drought or competition with neighbouring trees might be offsetting the benefit of extra growing time.

In this thesis, I used two datasets that track both seasonal timing and annual growth (measured through tree rings): one from young trees of four species planted together in a common garden, and another from a community science program monitoring eleven species of mature trees. Both studies were based in an urban arboretum, a setting with little effects of drought and competition, thus isolating the effect of season length itself.

For the young trees, I found that longer and warmer seasons did lead to more growth, regardless of where the trees' seed source originally came from. Interestingly, for most species the end of season mattered more than the timing of start of season in the spring, suggesting that when studying how growth responds to longer seasons, we shouldn't focus solely on spring.

For the mature trees, though, our results lined up with other recent findings: growth and season length appeared disconnected, regardless of each species' life-history strategy. I also found that growth for two species shifter when we considered the previous year's conditions, highlighting the need not to consider only one year of growing conditions. This points to growth patterns that might otherwise stay hidden, and hints that we need to better understand how trees allocate carbon between growth and storage.

Overall, our findings suggest that longer growing seasons under climate change could help forests regenerate and expand their range, but may actually reduce the amount of carbon temperate forests are able to store overall.
