Climate change shifts the timing of their plants' seasonal activities, such as when the leaves emerge in the spring and when they fall in autumn. One of the expected knock-on effects is increased tree growth with longer growing seasons since trees can grow for a longer period. This matters because tree growth is a major part of how forests pull carbon out of the atmosphere, and thus massively slow down the consequences of anthropogenic climate change. But several recent studies failed to connect growth and longer growing seasons, suggesting that other factors-like drought or competition with neighbouring trees might be offsetting the benefit of extra growing time.

In this thesis, I used two datasets that track both seasonal timing and annual growth (measured through tree rings): one from young trees of four species planted together in a common garden, and another from a community science program monitoring eleven species of mature trees. Both studies were based in an urban arboretum, a setting with little effects of drought and competition, thus isolating the effect of season length itself.

For the young trees, I found that longer and warmer seasons did lead to more growth, regardless of where the trees' seed source originally came from. Interestingly, for most species the end of season mattered more than the timing of start of season in the spring, suggesting that when studying how growth responds to longer seasons, we shouldn't focus solely on spring.

For the mature trees, though, our results lined up with other recent findings: growth and season length appeared disconnected, regardless of each species' life-history strategy. I also found that growth for two species shifter when we considered the previous year's conditions, highlighting the need not to consider only one year of growing conditions. This points to growth patterns that might otherwise stay hidden, and hints that we need to better understand how trees allocate carbon between growth and storage.

Overall, our findings suggest that longer growing seasons under climate change could help forests regenerate and expand their range, but may actually reduce the amount of carbon temperate forests are able to store overall.

Chapter 1

Climate change shifts the timing of plants' seasonal activities, such as when leaves emerge in spring and when they fall in autumn. One expected knock-on effect is increased tree growth with longer growing seasons, since trees can grow for a longer period. This matters because tree growth is a major part of how forests pull carbon out of the atmosphere, thus slowing down the consequences of anthropogenic climate change. But several recent studies failed to connect growth and longer growing seasons, suggesting that other factors—like drought or competition with neighbouring trees—might be offsetting the benefit of extra growing time.

To understand how manipulated spring and fall temperatures affect tree phenology in the first place, I conducted experiments in 2024 across seven species under controlled conditions, spanning fast/short-lived species (e.g., Populus balsamifera) to slow/long-lived species (e.g., Quercus macrocarpa), including both deciduous and evergreen trees. I used a full factorial design of spring and fall warming with two levels each (control/warmed), resulting in four treatments plus two additional treatments testing fall nutrient effects, with 15 replicates each (630 trees total). I tracked phenological events weekly throughout the 2024 growing season and built a Bayesian hierarchical model to compare responses across treatments and species. My preliminary results suggest very different responses across species, where fast-growing species seem more responsive to longer seasons than slow-growing ones. These results will help select urban tree species that best exploit longer growing seasons under climate change.

Chapter 2

Building on this, my second chapter asks how phenological phases regulate growth in both mature and juvenile trees in urban ecosystems. I used two datasets that track both seasonal timing and annual growth (measured through tree rings): one from young trees of four species planted together in a common garden, and another from a community science program monitoring eleven species of mature trees. Both studies were based in an urban arboretum, a setting with little effect from drought and competition, thus isolating the effect of season length itself.

For the young trees, I used five years of leaf phenology and tree cross-section data from the common garden to analyze how ring width relates to phenology across years, and found that longer and warmer seasons did lead to more growth, regardless of where the trees' seed source originally came from. Interestingly, for most species the end of season mattered more than the start of season in spring, suggesting that when studying how growth responds to longer seasons, we shouldn't focus solely on spring.

For the mature trees, I leveraged ten years of Treespotters phenology data and analyzed tree rings from 50 trees across 11 species with a Bayesian cross-design model linking growth rings to phenology. My results lined up with other recent findings: growth and season length appeared disconnected, regardless of each species' life-history strategy, and juvenile trees showed a much greater growth response to season length than mature trees—consistent with literature suggesting young trees exploit additional growing days more readily. I also found that growth for two species shifted when I considered the previous year's conditions, highlighting the need not to consider only one year of growing conditions. This points to growth patterns that might otherwise stay hidden, and hints that we need to better understand how trees allocate carbon between growth and storage.

Together, these findings suggest that longer growing seasons under climate change could help forests regenerate and expand their range, particularly for juvenile trees, but may reduce the overall carbon storage capacity of mature temperate forests — directly informing strategies for urban and natural forest planning under rapid climate change.
